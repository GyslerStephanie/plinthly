Read this with the sample in mind

This is one interview with one couple, and an unusually well‑equipped one: Zita is an architect (understands construction + subsidy rules), Jonas works in the federal sector (predictable salary). Their pain points likely understate what a less‑informed buyer feels. They're also self‑described pessimists about ever affording what they want. So: strong signals to test, not proven facts. The single biggest shift from my prior analysis is that one claim I'd marked INFER — rent‑vs‑buy / invest‑the‑difference — is now spoken evidence, in almost exactly the framing I'd hypothesized.

1

What they actually asked for

Nine themes condensed to the five that carry a product implication, each tagged by evidence strength.

The headline need — invert the calculator and add time Validated

The clearest, most repeated, unprompted frustration: existing tools take a price and return a binary yes/no. They want the opposite — affordability computed from their means, projected over years.

"I do not want to know if I can afford this right now or not. I want to know in 5 years or 10 years or 20 years."
"My salary is going to increase like this, we save this much per year. When would we be able to afford it — in one, two, three, five years?"

They even solved the hard part themselves: price growth can't be predicted, so make it a user‑set assumption (they floated 1–2%/yr), and watching it play out might reveal the target is never reachable — which is itself useful.

The sustainability wedge — opacity, rationally abandoned Validated Feasibility risk

They know subsidies / tax deductions / energy savings exist and could be decisive — and have written them off, because the info is fragmented to Gemeinde level and needs a concrete plan for a specific house.

"It is such a jungle… easier to make people pessimistic and say this is the price, assume you get no subsidies — even though the subsidies would in some cases be what makes it affordable."

But the bar for usefulness is low — directional advice is enough: "It does not even have to be accurate… many people do not realize you can save money this or that way." Zita (in the field) believes the rules will never standardize — that's the feasibility risk on this wedge.

Three more, briefly

  • Honesty, not positivity Validated — "Typically ChatGPT is always on your side… I want the cons also." Matches Plinthly's existing voice exactly.
  • "Surface the questions I don't know to ask" Signal — schools by Gemeinde, commute/car dependency, canton taxes, hidden maintenance. They called it "automatic prompt engineering."
  • An entry point for the blind newcomer Signal — "if you don't know what to study, you cannot self‑study"; existing foreigner guides have "horrible SEO" and are too long. Someone might "not even know what a second pillar is."

The headline need vs. what Plinthly already does

Good news: Plinthly is ~40% of the way to the inverted‑time calculator. It already inverts (Phase 1) and already shows a savings→timeline (PathToGoal). It's missing the two things that make it honest over a 5–20 yr horizon.

Citations: affordability.js, src/components/PathToGoal.jsx (monthsFor = equityGap / perMonth, fixed target), monthlyCostsAtRate.

⚠︎ Why this matters for honesty

The current timeline quietly cheats in two opposite directions: it ignores appreciation (so the target looks closer than it is) and ignores salary growth (so income‑bound buyers are told "saving won't help," when over 10 years it might). Adding both isn't feature‑creep — it's what makes the projection true, which is the whole brand.

Extend Phase 1 + PathToGoal into a real "when could we afford our target?" projection. New inputs: capital, monthly savings, income growth, risk‑buffer preference, and a user‑set price‑appreciation %. Output: affordable price now + a year‑by‑year timeline to the target.

◇ Personas this surfaced (for targeting the next interviews)

The patient pessimist (Zita & Jonas) — primed to own, blocked by the gap & opacity, long 15–20yr horizon. The blind newcomer — no finance/architecture network; for them the basics are the product. The investor‑only owner — buys abroad as a rental, never to live in; a different job entirely.

The honest read

The strongest, cleanest signal is the inverted, time‑aware calculator — a real unmet need, stated unprompted, in the user's own words — and Plinthly is already partway there: it inverts (Phase 1) and shows a savings timeline (PathToGoal), but against a static target and ignoring salary growth, which is exactly what makes a 5–20 year projection honest. Build that (F1/F2) first; it's an extension, not a rebuild. The sustainability wedge (F3) is genuinely needed and on‑mission but carries the heaviest feasibility risk (Gemeinde opacity) — treat it as a hypothesis to test, not a promise to build. Rent‑vs‑buy (F4) graduated from my prior inferred recommendation to spoken evidence. And the honesty‑as‑structure move (F7) is nearly free and already your voice. Verdict, in the synthesis's own words: a strong yellow — a real need worth a proper prototype, with two unknowns (subsidy feasibility, willingness to pay) to probe cheaply before committing more. And remember: this is n=1.
